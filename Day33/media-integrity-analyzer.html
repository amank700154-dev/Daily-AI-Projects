Challenge 01 · Headline Detective

Does the headline earn your click?

Headlines compete for attention. Sometimes that pressure turns a small detail into a dramatic claim. The goal is not to distrust every headline — it is to compare the headline with what the article actually supports.

Before you investigate

In everyday life, you might see a headline while scrolling between classes, messages, or videos. Pause for a moment: what is the headline promising, and does the evidence below really support that promise?

Challenge 02 · Emotion Detector

Notice what the words make you feel.

Emotional language is not automatically bad. But when fear, anger, outrage, or excitement is deliberately amplified, emotion can steer attention before we have time to evaluate the claim.

Before you react

Think about a post you might encounter in a group chat or social feed. Ask yourself: what emotion is this trying to create, who would respond strongly to it, and what action does that emotion make feel natural?

Biggest red flag

Look for claims that sound stronger than their evidence.

The strongest signal is often the gap between what a message makes you feel and what it can actually demonstrate.

Three practical media literacy habits

  1. Slow the click. Read beyond the headline before sharing or reacting.
  2. Separate facts from framing. Ask what is directly supported and what is interpretation.
  3. Name the emotion. If a message makes you instantly furious, frightened, or euphoric, pause and investigate why.

Replay with completely new scenarios

A single example can teach a pattern. Replaying the analyzer generates a different fictional story and a different emotional post so you can practice spotting the pattern again.
