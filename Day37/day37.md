## Stage 1 — Who Owns This?

Stage 1 focused on identifying the **primary owner** of a workplace task.

The important lesson was that ownership should usually be connected to the person or function with the clearest responsibility, expertise, and authority to move the work forward.

For example, a food-quality issue is closely connected to the kitchen, while a reservation-flow problem is closely connected to the host/front-desk function.

## Stage 2 — Task Routing

Stage 2 focused on building a workflow instead of assigning one person to everything.

I learned that a task can move through several roles. A practical workflow may look like:

**Identify → Coordinate → Investigate / Execute → Communicate → Follow up**

The exact order depends on the situation.

## Stage 3 — Collaboration Challenge

Stage 3 demonstrated that larger organizational problems usually require multiple teams.

For example, a sudden drop in customer satisfaction can involve:

- Restaurant Manager
- Service Team
- Head Chef
- Marketing

The manager can act as the primary coordinator while the other teams contribute information and actions within their areas.

## Key Learnings

### 1. Ownership is not the same as doing everything
A primary owner is responsible for coordinating progress, but does not necessarily perform every part of the task.

### 2. Delegation keeps work moving
Good delegation sends parts of the problem to people who have the appropriate expertise or authority.

### 3. Handoffs matter
Work can slow down when information does not move clearly between teams. A good workflow makes the next responsibility visible.

### 4. Complex problems need collaboration
Restaurant operations are interconnected. Kitchen, service, reservations, purchasing, finance, marketing, and management can all affect the same customer outcome.

### 5. Organizations are systems
A workplace works more like a network than a collection of isolated job titles. Understanding the relationships between roles is often more useful than memorizing titles.

## Organizational Insight

> Many real workplace problems are solved by teams rather than individuals. A clear owner can coordinate the work without doing every part of it.

## Final Reflection

The main takeaway from Task Compass is that effective organizations depend on **clear ownership, sensible delegation, useful handoffs, and collaboration**. Different roles contribute different kinds of expertise, so solving a workplace problem often means coordinating those contributions rather than expecting one person to handle everything.
